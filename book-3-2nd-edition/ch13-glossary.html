Numerical data

Data that are numbers. Discrete data is counted; continuous data is measured.

ExampleNumber of siblings (discrete). Height (continuous).

Categorical data

Data in categories, using words or labels. Nominal has no order; ordinal has a natural order.

ExampleEye colour (nominal). T-shirt size S, M, L (ordinal).

Primary data

Data you collect yourself, first-hand.

ExampleThe results of your own class survey.

Secondary data

Data that someone else has already collected.

ExampleCensus figures from the CSO.

Population

The whole group you want to find out about.

ExampleAll 5th Year students in Ireland.

Sample

A smaller group chosen from the population.

Example100 5th Year students from around the country.

Survey

Collecting data by asking a group of people questions.

ExampleAsking 50 students how they get to school.

Designed experiment

The researcher changes something (a treatment) and measures the effect, often against a control group.

ExampleHalf the plants get fertiliser, half don't, then compare growth.

Questionnaire

A set of written questions used to collect data in a survey. Good questions are clear, short and not leading.

ExampleLeading: ‘Don’t you agree school starts too early?’
Better: ‘What time should school start?’

Stem-and-leaf diagram

A table that sorts data by splitting each value into a stem (the first digit or digits) and a leaf (the last digit). It keeps every actual value.

ExampleKey: 2 | 7 = 27
21, 24 and 27 go on stem 2 as leaves 1 4 7.

Scatter graph

A graph of paired data, plotted as points, to see if two variables are related.

ExampleHeight on the x-axis against arm span on the y-axis.

Correlation

How closely two variables follow a straight-line pattern: positive, negative or none. It is measured from −1 to +1.

ExampleHeight and arm span have a strong positive correlation (r close to 1).

Histogram

A bar chart for continuous data grouped into class intervals. The bars touch.

ExampleHeights grouped 150 to 160 cm, 160 to 170 cm, and so on.

Skewed (right or left)

A distribution with a long tail on one side. Skewed right (positive) has the tail on the right; skewed left (negative) has it on the left.

ExampleIncomes are skewed right. Marks on a very easy test are skewed left.
